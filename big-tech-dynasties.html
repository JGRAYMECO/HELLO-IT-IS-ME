Big Tech Dynasties

Rethinking the Power, Spectacle, and Downfall of Google, Apple, Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon

Big Tech is dynastic, and because it is dynastic, Big Tech is inherently undemocratic. Big Tech Dynasties explains how five companies came to exercise power over economic, social and political life, and what democratic governments can do to take it back.

What is Big Tech Dynasties about?

Google, Apple, Microsoft, Meta and Amazon shape how we work, communicate, shop, learn and take part in public life. They are products of platform capitalism, firms that grew by capturing network effects and data. Big Tech Dynasties shows that they also follow a far older pattern of power: the dynasty.

The book traces how each company built and sustains its dominance, the spectacle that surrounds it, and the forces that could bring about its decline. It ends with democratic pushback, highlighting Australia’s role as an unexpected leader in overseeing Big Tech, and proposes an agenda other nations can use to regain democratic control.

Why “dynasties”?

Dynasties are defined by how they hold on to power. The book identifies three mechanisms through which Big Tech firms endure:

  • Economic control of data, platforms and cloud computing, the infrastructure on which others depend
  • Legitimating ideology: founder myths and an ideology of innovation that make private power seem natural and deserved
  • Succession: the capacity to carry power across leadership changes and generations of technology

Thinking dynastically puts the focus on accountability: to whom, if anyone, is this power answerable?

How does Big Tech exercise governing power?

Big Tech companies set the rules of the markets they compete in. Through the design of platforms, app stores, search, cloud services and AI systems, they decide what is visible, what is permitted, who can reach customers and on what terms. This is private governing power: rule-making, enforcement and adjudication carried out by companies, outside the accountability that applies to public institutions. Because governments, businesses and citizens increasingly depend on their infrastructure, these companies can also shape the conditions under which states themselves govern.

Google, Apple, Microsoft, Meta and Amazon

The book examines each company as its own dynasty, with a distinctive foundation of power, origin story and line of succession, while showing the common pattern that unites them. Together they control much of the world’s search, mobile operating systems, enterprise software, social media, e-commerce and cloud infrastructure, and they are now positioning themselves to dominate artificial intelligence and wearable computing.

What can democratic governments do?

Big Tech Dynasties argues that competition law is one tool among several. Democracies need the capacity to govern technology in the public interest: to understand the infrastructure they depend on, to set enforceable rules and to coordinate with other nations. Australia’s experience, from the News Media Bargaining Code to online safety and digital platform regulation, shows both what a mid-sized democracy can achieve and where its limits lie.

This argument connects to my research on digital sovereignty and AI infrastructure.

About the author

Associate Professor Joanne E. Gray is a scholar of digital technology governance and political economy, and Chair of Discipline in Media and Communications. She is the author of Google Rules: The History and Future of Copyright Under the Influence of Google (Oxford University Press, 2020), Editor-in-Chief of Policy & Internet, and Lead Chief Investigator of the ARC Discovery Project Governing Immersive Tech. Her work has informed Australian regulators and legislation on digital platforms and online safety.
