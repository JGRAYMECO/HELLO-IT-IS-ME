Big Tech and Private Power

A handful of technology companies now set many of the rules for markets, information and public life. My research examines how Google, Apple, Microsoft, Meta and Amazon acquire and keep that power, how they legitimise it, and what democracies can do to hold it to account.

What is private power?

Big Tech companies compete in markets, and they also make the rules within them. Through the design of platforms, app stores, search, cloud services and AI systems, they decide what is visible, what is permitted, who can reach customers and on what terms. I call this private governing power: rule-making, enforcement and adjudication carried out by companies rather than by accountable public institutions.

Because governments, businesses and citizens increasingly depend on their infrastructure, these companies can also shape the conditions under which states themselves govern. My work traces how this power is built, how it is justified, and how it extends into new technologies, from search and social media to extended reality and AI.

Books

Big Tech Dynasties

Bloomsbury, 2027

Google, Apple, Microsoft, Meta and Amazon are usually explained as products of platform capitalism. Big Tech Dynasties argues that they also follow a far older pattern of power: the dynasty. The book shows how each company sustains its dominance through economic control of data, platforms and cloud computing; through founder myths and an ideology of innovation; and through succession across leadership changes and generations of technology. It ends with an agenda for democratic oversight, drawing on Australia’s experience as an unexpected leader in regulating Big Tech.

Google Rules: The History and Future of Copyright Under the Influence of Google

Oxford University Press, 2020

A history of how one company came to shape the rules of copyright. Google Rules traces Google’s influence on copyright law and policy through litigation, lobbying and its own private systems for managing copyright, such as YouTube’s Content ID, and asks what this means for creators, users and the public interest. It is an early account of a technology company acting as a private governor of information.

Selected articles

Big tech trajectories: A layer-model approach to extensions of platform power in the emerging XR economy

Bay & Gray, Internet Policy Review, 2026

Meta, Apple, Microsoft, Google, Amazon, ByteDance and Tencent are positioning themselves to dominate extended reality (XR). This article proposes a layer model of the XR economy, from network infrastructure through software to user experience, so regulators can see where power concentrates across the whole technology stack. Case studies show three different routes to dominance: Meta through vertical integration, Apple through ecosystem control, and Microsoft through infrastructural reach. Regulation focused on single markets or products will miss these cross-layer concentrations of power.

Experts React—The Politics of Technology in Trump’s Second Term

Gray, Policy & Internet, 2026

An editorial collection in which scholars from four continents respond to technology politics under Trump’s second term: the prominence of tech executives in government, expanded surveillance, large AI deals, the rise of crypto in policy and the state restructuring of social media markets. Contributors consider the spread of digital authoritarianism, global reliance on US-owned communication technologies and the openings this moment may create for the Global South.

The geopolitics of ‘platforms’: The TikTok challenge

Gray, Internet Policy Review, 2021

The 2020 controversy over TikTok in the United States was framed largely as a national security threat tied to rivalry with China. Analysing government and corporate statements, this article argues that the geopolitical framing obscured a more important issue: competition in a highly concentrated global platform market dominated by US companies. TikTok’s success showed that users will adopt new platforms whatever their origin, which points to antitrust enforcement and data interoperability as better responses.

China’s evolving stance against tech monopolies: A moment of international alignment in an era of digital sovereignty

Wang & Gray, Media International Australia, 2022

Tracing China’s anti-monopoly actions against its largest platform companies and comparing them with the European Union’s digital sovereignty agenda, this article finds that in both jurisdictions digital sovereignty is driven by concern about the excessive power of platform companies as well as by rivalry between states. Monopoly concerns emerge as a modest but overlooked point of common ground between the EU and China.

Democracy in the digital era

Gray, Hutchinson & Stilinovic, Policy & Internet, 2023

An editorial introducing a Policy & Internet issue on the relationship between digital platforms and democracy, and the policy questions it raises for governments, platforms and citizens.
