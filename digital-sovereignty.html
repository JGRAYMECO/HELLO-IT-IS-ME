Digital Sovereignty and AI Infrastructure

Australia’s economy, public services and democratic institutions increasingly run on digital infrastructure owned and operated by a small number of global technology companies. My research asks what this dependence means for a country’s capacity to govern, and how that capacity can be built.

What is digital sovereignty?

Digital sovereignty is the capacity of a political community to make, enforce and oversee the rules that govern the digital technologies and infrastructures it depends on. Few countries can build every technology they rely on, and control over data is only one part of the problem. In my work, digital sovereignty is therefore a question of governance: whether a state can still govern effectively when the systems its society relies on are owned, operated and controlled by others, including powerful private companies. Seen this way, digital sovereignty is shaped as much by the concentrated power of global technology companies as by rivalry between nation-states.

Digital sovereignty in Australia

Australia faces a distinctive dependency problem. It is a wealthy, highly digitised democracy, but a relatively small market that largely relies on foreign-owned platforms, cloud services and AI models. Government agencies, businesses, schools and hospitals rely heavily on services provided by a few, mostly US-based, companies. Recent multi-billion-dollar data centre investments by global hyperscalers deepen that relationship, even as they are presented as building national capability.

Australia has also been an unexpected leader in pushing back. Through measures such as the News Media Bargaining Code, the ACCC’s Digital Platform Services Inquiry and online safety regulation, it has tested how far a mid-sized democracy can govern global technology companies. The question now is whether that governing capacity can be extended to the infrastructure layer, where dependence is deepest and least visible.

AI infrastructure and sovereignty

Artificial intelligence rests on a stack of physical and digital infrastructure, and dependence can arise at every layer:

  • Subsea cables and networks that carry data in and out of the country
  • Data centres and energy, the physical sites where data is stored and processed
  • Compute and chips, the specialised hardware needed to train and run AI models
  • Cloud platforms that rent this capacity to governments and businesses
  • Data, including where it is held, under which jurisdiction and who can access it
  • AI models, mostly developed offshore and accessed through commercial terms
  • Applications and platforms through which AI reaches citizens and institutions

These dependencies matter because control over infrastructure can become a form of governing power. The companies that own these layers can set terms, shape what is technically possible and influence policy, often beyond the reach of national regulators. Every country depends on others; what matters is whether a country keeps the capacity to set conditions, scrutinise and change course.

What is sovereign governing capacity?

Sovereign governing capacity is a state’s practical ability to govern under conditions of technological dependence. It means being able to make rules, enforce them and exercise oversight over digital infrastructure that is largely owned and operated by others. The test it sets is “Can Australia still govern this technology?”, whoever owns it.

Sovereign governing capacity can be gained or lost. It is weakened when dependencies lock governments into particular providers, when critical knowledge sits only inside companies, or when the cost of enforcing rules becomes prohibitive. It is strengthened through procurement conditions, technical expertise within government, regulatory coordination, regional partnerships and design choices made before dependencies set in.

What is layered digital sovereignty?

Layered digital sovereignty is a framework for analysing sovereignty layer by layer across the technology stack, from physical infrastructure to applications. It recognises that a country’s dependence, risks and capacity to govern are different at each layer. Australia may have strong regulatory reach over platforms and applications but very little over chips or foundation models.

The framework builds on a layer model of platform power I developed with Morten Bay for the immersive technology economy (Bay & Gray, 2026, Internet Policy Review), extending it to Australia’s digital infrastructure as a whole. A layered approach also shows how dominant firms extend control across layers. I call this infrastructural capture: control over one layer is used to entrench power in others.

Current research

Sovereignty by Design is my developing research programme on democratic governance of Australia’s AI infrastructure. It maps dependencies and sovereignty risks across the AI infrastructure stack, develops a theory of layered digital sovereignty, and translates it into practical principles that governments can use to build sovereign governing capacity before dependencies become locked in.

Related work

  • Asia Pacific Digital Sovereignty Collective: a regional network of researchers working on digital sovereignty across diverse political contexts, with current priorities in artificial intelligence, critical digital infrastructures, and media and cultural industries.
