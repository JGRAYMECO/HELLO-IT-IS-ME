Smart Glasses, Privacy and Consent in Australia

Smart glasses make it easy to record people without them knowing. Our research shows how this is already being used to film and harass women, and where Australian law falls short. This page answers the most common questions, drawing on that evidence.

By Associate Professor Joanne E. Gray, Lead Chief Investigator, ARC Discovery Project Governing Immersive Tech (DP250100343) · Updated October 2026

What are the privacy risks of smart glasses?

Smart glasses put a camera, microphone and, increasingly, an AI assistant at eye level. They make recording almost invisible. The main privacy risks are:

  • Covert recording. People are filmed without knowing, so they cannot consent, object or leave.
  • Continuous, incidental capture. Everyone in the wearer’s field of view can be recorded, including bystanders who never interact with the wearer.
  • Onward sharing. Footage is easily uploaded to social media, where it can attract abuse and expose personal details. This is a second layer of harm after the original encounter.
  • Data flows to companies. Recordings and voice queries are typically routed through a phone app and cloud services. Who can access them, where they are stored and how they are used is often unclear. In October 2026 the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner opened a formal investigation into the maker of the HeyCyan app used with low-cost smart glasses sold in Australia.
  • Identification. Combining wearable cameras with facial recognition or AI search could let wearers identify strangers in real time, creating risks of doxing and stalking.

Can smart glasses record people without consent in Australia?

Often, yes. There is no general right not to be filmed in public in Australia. Whether a particular recording is unlawful depends on where it happens, what is captured and what is done with it:

  • State and territory surveillance devices laws restrict recording private conversations and, in some jurisdictions, filming private activities. The rules differ between states. For example, some allow a person who is part of a conversation to record it.
  • Criminal laws cover harassment, stalking, voyeurism and the recording or sharing of intimate images, as well as using a phone or internet service to menace, harass or cause offence.
  • The eSafety Commissioner can act on image-based abuse and serious adult cyber abuse, and industry codes require platforms to deal with unlawful material.
  • The statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, in force since June 2025, allows people to sue for serious, intentional or reckless intrusions on their seclusion or misuse of private information.

Much of what our research documents (covertly filming a woman in a gym, on the street or at the beach and posting it for views) can fall between these frameworks.

This is general information about the regulatory landscape and does not constitute legal advice. If you have been recorded without consent, the eSafety Commissioner and your state or territory police can advise on options.

Why are smart glasses different from smartphones?

A smartphone has to be held up to record. That gesture is a social signal: people can see they are being filmed and respond. Smart glasses remove the signal. Recording is hands-free, at eye level and usually marked only by a small LED that is easy to miss and can be covered or disabled. A cottage industry of workarounds has emerged to defeat these safeguards.

The result is a shift in the default. With phones, recording is visible unless someone works to hide it. With smart glasses, recording is hidden unless the wearer chooses to disclose it. Laws and social norms built around visible cameras do not anticipate this.

What is ambient capture?

Ambient capture is the term our team uses for the covert filming of people, particularly women, in everyday settings using smart glasses, with the footage then posted online for engagement and entertainment. It is ambient because the recording blends into ordinary life. There is no visible camera and no moment at which the person filmed is asked.

How are smart glasses being used to harass women?

Our team analysed 350 publicly available Instagram videos posted between 2023 and 2026, focusing on discreet point-of-view recording. We found men using smart glasses to film approaches to women, often to obtain a phone number or social media handle, and then sharing the footage for views. Much of this content is connected to pick-up artist communities.

  • In a subset of covert point-of-view videos, about 60% of interactions were classified as potential harassment. Many women showed visible unease or tried to leave.
  • In about 43% of videos, the women filmed faced further abusive comments online, and some had personal details revealed.
  • Filming took place at work, at the gym, on the street, in apartment stairwells and at the beach.

The eSafety Commissioner has identified similar patterns, noting that covert filming of women reinforces the idea that women in public can be watched and controlled.

What does Australian privacy law cover?

The Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) regulates how organisations, including device makers, app developers and platforms, handle personal information. It is the basis for the OAIC’s current investigation into a smart-glasses app developer. But it has significant limits for this problem:

  • It generally does not apply to individuals acting in a personal capacity, so it does not regulate the person wearing the glasses.
  • Many smaller businesses are exempt.
  • Its consent rules govern how companies collect data; they say nothing about whether a bystander agreed to be filmed.

The second tranche of Privacy Act reforms, released as an exposure draft in 2026, would introduce a “fair and reasonable” test for data handling, but it does not directly address bystander recording by individuals. The statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy is the main new avenue that reaches individual wearers, though it requires a serious invasion and court action by the person affected.

What regulatory changes are needed?

Smart glasses are still at an early stage of adoption, which gives governments a critical opportunity to act before these harms become normalised. Our research points to three priorities:

  1. Clear consent standards for recording in public and semi-public spaces, recognising that covert, continuous capture is different in kind from a visible photograph. This is the case for a right not to be recorded.
  2. Platform accountability. Moderation systems are designed to catch clear, visible violations, so covertly recorded encounters posted as “dating advice” slip through. Platforms that host and profit from this content should be required to address it.
  3. Design requirements for wearables, such as recording indicators that cannot be covered or disabled and that stop recording if tampered with, so people can reliably tell when they are being recorded.
